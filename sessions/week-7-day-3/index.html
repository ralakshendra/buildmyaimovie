PASS 01

Diagnose and protect the project

Choose the edit that needs finishing and protect its original.

Bring in: An earlier film, advertisement or current in-class footage; editable project, source clips, dialogue, music and approved graphics.

  1. Watch the rough cut once without stopping. Write the intended feeling, message and ending, then mark the moments where attention, continuity or sound breaks.
  2. Save a separate working version before changing the timeline. Create media bins for footage, voice, music, SFX and graphics; keep the originals in a stable folder.
  3. Confirm the intended frame shape and project settings before importing more clips. Preserve those settings when Filmora offers to match an imported clip. Choose a layout you can work in; layout does not change the export shape.

Why: Finishing starts with a clear problem, not an effect. A saved baseline makes your improvement visible.

Expected result: Protected rough cut, organized working project and a timestamped edit diagnosis.

Review: The original still opens, required media is online and the chosen project has a clear intended result.

First fix: If media is offline, relink the original files before making new cuts. If the canvas is wrong, check Project Settings before scaling every shot.

Carry forward: Carry the organized media and diagnosis into the selection pass.

Source: Workshop structure; project saving and bins: Week 3 Day 2, 01:50–01:54.

PASS 02

Select usable frames and build the story

Use the strongest part of each generated clip and make the sequence understandable.

Bring in: Organized source footage, the intended story or message, and the protected rough cut.

  1. Preview each clip in the media bin. Mark In and Out around the usable action; discard hesitation, frozen tails and visible AI deformation.
  2. Assemble the opening, development and payoff in the order the viewer needs. Trim or split clips, then close unintended gaps. Inspect any ripple edit across the affected video and audio tracks.
  3. Cut before a camera move dies when momentum is the intention. A deliberate reaction or emotional hold may need more time; do not cut every shot to one fixed length.
  4. Play each join at normal speed, then inspect the frames either side. Check subject position, gaze, prop identity, motion direction and whether the action still makes sense.

Why: A generated clip's full duration is not its useful duration. Cutting on a continuing movement can carry energy through a hard cut.

Expected result: A coherent assembly with clean clip boundaries and no accidental black frames.

Review: Every shot adds information or feeling; the opening moves promptly and the ending resolves the message.

First fix: Trim around a local glitch, use a compatible alternate or insert a motivated cutaway. Regenerate only when the essential action is missing or unusable.

Carry forward: Carry the clean assembly into the continuity and timing passes.

Source: Week 3 Day 2, 01:54–02:02; B3 Week 4 Day 2 B3, 00:08–00:09 and 00:39–00:40.

PASS 03

Repair motion and connect shots

Repair fixable movement without making the scene less believable.

Bring in: The assembly and any alternate angles or cutaways already available.

  1. Compare the end of one shot with the next shot's opening. Find a shared movement, shape, light cue or action to motivate the join.
  2. Use Reverse only when the action still looks physically plausible. The source reverses a rising coconut to make it fall; inspect the trimmed result rather than assuming reversal fixes everything.
  3. Use a small Uniform Speed adjustment for sluggish movement when it supports the scene. Preview before committing; faster can reveal shaking or unnatural gestures.
  4. For dialogue, preserve the spoken phrase and choose a compatible close-up, reaction or wide shot. If the wrong character visibly speaks, cover only with footage that preserves meaning and sync; regenerate an essential speaking shot if necessary.

Why: Editing can remove a bad section or change its timing. It cannot reconstruct a missing action or repair every identity change.

Expected result: A continuity pass with each retained repair explained.

Review: No backward speech, implausible physics, visible sync error or new jump in position is introduced.

First fix: Undo the repair and compare an alternate shot. Return to generation when the defect occupies the action the audience must see.

Carry forward: Carry a believable sequence into rhythm work.

Source: Week 3 Day 2, 02:04–02:10; early dialogue coverage: Week 2 Day 1, 02:05.

PASS 04

Shape rhythm and speed ramps

Control energy while protecting the action the viewer needs to read.

Bring in: A clean sequence and music that suits its mood.

  1. Listen for the musical phrase and accents. In current Filmora desktop guidance, right-click the imported music in the media library and run Beat Detection, then place the analyzed file on the timeline. Menu locations vary by version; the recording uses Smart Edit Tool.
  2. Choose meaningful cuts or reveals to align with useful markers. Check by ear; a beat marker is a timing aid, not an instruction to cut on every beat.
  3. Select a suitable moving clip and open Speed > Speed Ramping. Compare a preset with Customize. Keep the important reveal readable and use faster sections to travel between moments.
  4. Compare the ramp with the original at normal playback. Adjust neighboring joins after retiming; separate clips onto tracks when needed to prevent unwanted movement, and distinguish snapping from magnetic timeline/linkage controls.

Why: The product demo alternates slower and faster sections to connect shots. Its 1×/5× example is not a universal recipe: direction, composition and timing still decide whether the cut works.

Expected result: A rhythm pass and either a useful ramp comparison or a recorded reason to retain natural speed.

Review: The important action reads clearly, joins feel intentional, and the edit has no new gaps or overlaps.

First fix: For stuttering, reduce the slowdown or keep 1× motion. Low-frame-rate AI footage does not gain true captured detail by changing export FPS. Optional Optical Flow may help, but inspect edges for warping and export a short test.

Carry forward: Carry the locked timing into animation and sound.

Source: B3 Week 4 Day 2 B3, 02:08–02:21 and 02:31–02:35; Week 3 Day 2, 02:02–02:03.

PASS 05

Animate graphics with purpose

Introduce a title, logo or image without distracting from the film.

Bring in: Locked timing, a permitted transparent graphic or readable title, and the intended ending.

  1. Place the graphic above the picture on its own video track. Check its scale and placement inside safe margins.
  2. Add a keyframe at the intended end state, then an earlier one at the starting state. Change scale or position to create a modest movement.
  3. For a fade-in, set the first opacity to 0 and the later opacity to 100. Preview the full entrance and hold long enough for the message to read.
  4. The source adds Color Glow sparingly to integrate a logo. Treat it as an optional effect, check whether your asset/effect is included in your plan, and compare with the clean version.

Why: Keyframes describe a change over time. Every animation should direct attention toward the message rather than compete with it.

Expected result: A readable graphic or end frame with deliberate timing.

Review: No title is clipped, distorted, rushed or obscuring the subject; the design still works on a phone.

First fix: Reduce movement, simplify the graphic or remove the effect. Recheck first and last keyframes if the element jumps.

Carry forward: Carry the settled title timing into the sound pass.

Source: B3 Week 4 Day 2 B3, 02:03–02:07; safe margins and opacity: Week 3 Day 2, 01:55–01:57.

PASS 06

Build sound in layers

Make the action and space believable while keeping the mix clear.

Bring in: The settled picture edit, dialogue if present, music, ambience and action-specific sound effects.

  1. Decide whether the scene needs music, SFX or both. Search for the actual action or setting, not just a generic cinematic sound; the source uses bus ambience and cinematic whooshes.
  2. Keep voice, music and SFX on separate audio tracks. Mute unwanted generated audio only after checking whether it contains useful dialogue or ambience.
  3. Trim each effect to the action and align its attack with the visible event. Use short fades where needed to avoid abrupt entries or exits; remove sound bleeding into an unrelated shot.
  4. Balance by listening, using clip volume or volume keyframes. Lower music and impacts that compete with speech. Check on headphones and an ordinary speaker.

Why: A quiet, well-placed effect can add more depth than a loud whoosh. The source explicitly reviews a poor SFX bump as something to improve.

Expected result: A layered mix with clear speech, motivated effects and controlled transitions.

Review: Important words remain understandable, no accidental doubled audio plays, and effects support their visible cause.

First fix: Solo the offending track, shorten or replace the effect, lower its gain and reshape the fade before adding more layers.

Carry forward: Carry the mix into sound bridges and dialogue refinement.

Source: Week 3 Day 2, 02:11–02:16; B3 Week 4 Day 2 B3, 01:59–02:02 and 02:35–02:37.

PASS 07

Bridge scenes and protect dialogue

Use sound to connect scenes and make speech easy to follow.

Bring in: A layered mix, two neighboring scenes and dialogue where applicable.

  1. For a J-cut, let the incoming scene's sound start before its picture. For an L-cut, let the outgoing sound continue after the picture changes.
  2. Detach audio where independent timing is needed, then inspect sync across the remaining speaking sections. Keep a copy before changing linked media.
  3. For dialogue, lower background music with manual volume keyframes or select the main voice clip and enable Audio Ducking in its Audio properties if available. Listen to the amount and recovery; do not assume the default is right.
  4. Optional cleanup: compare mild noise reduction with bypass. Stop if speech becomes thin, metallic or loses consonants. Preserve useful room tone between lines.

Why: A sound bridge can carry a thought or introduce a place before the picture arrives. In a music-led ad with no dialogue, refine ambience and transitions instead of inventing narration.

Expected result: A motivated sound bridge, or an explained clean-cut choice, plus an intelligible mix.

Review: No words are clipped, mouth movement remains in sync and music returns naturally after speech.

First fix: Shorten the overlap or use ambience rather than incompatible dialogue. Reduce ducking or cleanup if it pumps or damages the voice.

Carry forward: Carry the balanced mix into finishing.

Source: J/L cut explanation: Week 3 Day 2, 02:14–02:16. Ducking and cleanup are supplementary workshop guidance.

PASS 08

Match colour and finish text

Make neighboring shots feel connected and ensure text is useful.

Bring in: The edited sequence, a representative reference shot and any required titles or captions.

  1. Compare adjacent shots before adding a look. In Color > Basic, adjust exposure and white balance, then contrast and saturation conservatively; judge the scene as a sequence.
  2. Use a chosen reference shot when matching. If available, compare Color Match or AI Color Palette with a manual correction; reduce the effect when skin, product colour or whites shift unnaturally.
  3. Add a restrained creative look after the matching pass. Check dark detail and highlights rather than applying a strong preset to hide mismatches.
  4. Proofread titles and any captions against the actual speech. Correct names, punctuation and timing. Use captions when they serve the audience; do not add fictional speech to a silent film.

Why: Correction makes shots agree; grading shapes the mood. Captions and titles should remain readable without covering faces, products or important action.

Expected result: A matched finishing pass and checked on-screen text.

Review: Adjacent shots have a consistent intended mood, subjects retain plausible colour and all retained text is readable and accurate.

First fix: Reset the strongest preset, choose a better reference and correct the individual shot. Simplify text or reposition it inside the safe area.

Carry forward: Carry the finished picture and text into export review.

Source: Supplementary finishing guidance, supported by current Filmora colour and title documentation; not a claimed transcript demonstration.

PASS 09

Export and inspect the actual file

Deliver a playable file that matches the intended project.

Bring in: The final timeline, destination requirements and the licensed media used in the edit.

  1. Scrub the full timeline for gaps, stray frames, accidental disabled clips and unwanted tails. Confirm the intended start/end range.
  2. Check audio is included, name the file clearly and choose the output folder. The source demonstrates MP4, H.264 and AAC with 1080p; use destination-appropriate resolution and the intended project frame rate rather than treating a historical example as mandatory.
  3. Check any premium media/effect warnings before export. An editor subscription does not necessarily include every creative asset. Replace unavailable items or use properly licensed alternatives.
  4. Export and open the file outside Filmora. Watch from start to finish with sound, including the first/last frames, fast motion, text and all repaired joins. Compare against the timeline.

Why: A project file is not the exported movie. Playback, watermark and range errors often become visible only in the actual output.

Expected result: A checked final film and saved editable project with linked media.

Review: The file opens, includes its intended sound and ending, has no accidental black frames or watermark, and uses the expected frame shape.

First fix: If preview is choppy, Render Preview first and compare a short export. If the exported file fails, isolate the failing range or asset, correct it and re-export; never mark the review complete from preview alone.

Carry forward: Carry the checked export, rough cut and evidence into the homework handoff.

Source: Week 3 Day 2, 02:16–02:21; licensing/export issues: B3 Week 4 Day 2 B3, 02:35–02:38.

PASS 10

Compare, explain and hand off

Show the improvement and leave the project ready for its next stage.

Bring in: Protected rough cut, checked final export and evidence from the applicable workshop tasks.

  1. Compare the rough and final versions at the same moments. Explain the editing decisions that changed clarity, emotion, rhythm or sound.
  2. Complete the applicable task fields in the Homework Doc; mark a technique N/A with a reason when your film does not need it.
  3. Save the final film, editable project and media location. Record any unresolved issue and the next useful correction.
  4. Use the current cohort submission instructions for hand-in. This session has no separately supplied upload destination or deadline. Website checkmarks record local practice, not submission receipt.

Why: A good finishing decision is something you can explain and repeat on a different project.

Expected result: Completed homework evidence and an organized film ready for review or the later portfolio.

Review: Rough cut, final film and project backup are traceable; the comparison identifies concrete improvements and honest limitations.

First fix: Replace a general claim such as 'more cinematic' with a timestamp, visible change and reason. Check links before sharing through the instructed channel.

Carry forward: Use the checked film as your next review or portfolio candidate.

Go deeper where your edit needs it

Optional editing clinics, adapted from the supplied editing handbook and the workshop techniques. Choose a clinic to solve a visible problem; these are not additional compulsory deliverables.

Choose a transition for a reason

Begin with a clean cut. Use a dissolve for a meaningful passage of time or softer connection, a fade for an opening or ending, and a match cut for related shapes or actions. A jump cut intentionally compresses time. Compare each treatment against the clean cut; avoid adding a transition merely to conceal incompatible action.

Eye trace, screen direction and inserts

Check where the viewer is looking before and after the cut. Keep important action in a readable area and preserve left/right relationships between characters. Use an available detail shot or reaction as a motivated insert when it improves clarity. A cutaway can cover a trim, but must still belong to the same moment and story.

Trim, ripple and rolling decisions

A normal trim changes a clip edge; a ripple trim also shifts later timing to close or open space. A rolling edit moves a shared boundary while keeping the combined duration. In Filmora, use the controls available in your installed version to achieve the intended result and inspect the other tracks. Do not copy the handbook's editor-specific shortcut letters into Filmora without checking its shortcut settings.

Masks, overlays and grouped clips

Use a mask or split screen only when a region or simultaneous view serves the story. Inspect the boundary through the full movement, with enough feathering to avoid a hard seam. Group or compound related clips when your version supports it, after saving a separate project version. Inspect inside the group before applying a global change. Detailed VFX work belongs in the next class.

Clean audio without flattening the performance

Find the problem first: rumble, harshness, background noise or uneven level. Compare a small EQ, noise-reduction or dynamics adjustment with bypass if available. Listen for lost consonants, pumping and metallic texture. Set the voice first, then the surrounding music and effects. The destination's delivery specification determines level targets; there is no universal slider value for every film.

Make the project easy to reopen

Keep footage, voice, music, SFX, graphics, exports and project files in clear folders. Use descriptive version names, retain the last known-good export and keep an independent backup. Test reopening and relinking before handing a project to someone else.

Supporting reference: Manifesto Editorum — The Editor's Handbook by Harry Ranveer, supplied in the cohort editing materials. Its examples describe general editing concepts; installed Filmora controls and the source demonstrations govern this workshop.

Judge the film, not the effects count

  • checked the project frame shape and retained only usable footage ranges.
  • reviewed shot order, motion continuity and every repaired join.
  • made a deliberate rhythm decision; ramps or natural speed support the scene.
  • checked keyframed graphics or the chosen static treatment for clarity and safe placement.
  • balanced the required audio layers and verified speech or ambience transitions.
  • checked colour consistency and all retained titles or captions.
  • watched the actual exported file from beginning to end with sound.
